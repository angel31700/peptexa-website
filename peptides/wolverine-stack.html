What the Wolverine Stack is -- and where it comes from

The "Wolverine Stack" is a popular name for the combination of two peptides: BPC-157 (Body Protection Compound-157) and TB-500 (a fragment of Thymosin Beta-4). The name references the X-Men character known for rapid tissue regeneration -- which gives you a sense of the hype level. Let's look at what the data actually shows.

BPC-157 is a 15-amino-acid peptide derived from a protein found in human gastric juice. TB-500 is a synthetic fragment of Thymosin Beta-4, a naturally occurring 43-amino-acid protein involved in tissue repair and cell migration. Both have extensive animal data. Neither has published human RCT data. The combination has no clinical data at all.

The Wolverine Stack is a community-coined term, not a clinical protocol. The rationale -- combining complementary repair mechanisms -- is pharmacologically reasonable. But the evidence base is individual-compound animal data extrapolated to a combination used in humans. That's a meaningful gap.

Mechanism, in plain English

The theoretical rationale for combining BPC-157 and TB-500 is complementary mechanisms: one works through growth factor signaling and angiogenesis, the other through cell migration and inflammation reduction.

What the human evidence shows

BPC-157 has been studied extensively in animal models. Sikiric et al. (2010) reviewed decades of research showing BPC-157's effects on tendon, ligament, muscle, bone, and GI tissue healing in animals. The consistency of animal data across multiple injury types is notable.

TB-500 (Thymosin Beta-4) has a separate body of animal literature. Goldstein et al. (2012) reviewed its role in wound healing, cardiac repair, and anti-inflammatory processes. Thymosin Beta-4 has been investigated in human clinical trials for wound healing and dry eye, though TB-500 is a specific fragment used in the peptide community.

The combination itself has no published clinical or preclinical data. The "stack" rationale is based on complementary mechanisms from individual compound studies. Evidence level for the combination: ★★☆☆☆ -- based on individual compound animal data only.

The individual compounds have real data -- BPC-157 in particular has a remarkably consistent animal evidence base. But 'both compounds work individually in animals' does not automatically mean 'combining them in humans is safe and effective.' That extrapolation hasn't been tested.

Peptexa editorial note

Regulatory status

Neither BPC-157 nor TB-500 is FDA-approved for any indication. Both are research compounds. In 2023, the FDA added BPC-157 to its list of compounds that cannot be used in compounding, further restricting access.

Thymosin Beta-4 (the parent molecule of TB-500) has been investigated in FDA-regulated clinical trials for wound healing. TB-500 specifically is not approved anywhere. The "Wolverine Stack" combination has no regulatory status in any country. Last reviewed 2026-08-19.

Common synonyms researchers use

Wolverine Stack  ·  BPC-157 + TB-500  ·  Body Protection Compound-157 + Thymosin Beta-4 fragment  ·  Repair stack  ·  Recovery stack. BPC-157 is sometimes called "Bepecin" in older literature.

Safety & adverse-event landscape

BPC-157 has demonstrated a favorable safety profile in published animal studies, with no reported lethal dose (LD1) in toxicology studies. TB-500, as a fragment of the endogenous protein Thymosin Beta-4, has theoretical safety advantages. However, neither compound has undergone rigorous human safety monitoring.

The primary safety concern with BPC-157 involves its angiogenic (blood vessel promoting) properties -- a theoretical concern for anyone with a history of cancer or active malignancy. TB-500 similarly promotes cell migration. The combination's safety profile is completely unstudied. Consult your provider.
