Matrixyl -- what the science actually shows

One of the most researched cosmetic peptides. Matrixyl mimics a collagen fragment that tells fibroblasts to make more collagen, fibronectin, and hyaluronic acid. Applied topically, not injected.

What Matrixyl is

Matrixyl is the trade name for palmitoyl pentapeptide-4, developed by Sederma. Five amino acids (KTTKS) linked to palmitic acid for skin penetration. The KTTKS sequence is a fragment of type I procollagen. Several generations: original Matrixyl, Matrixyl 3000, and Matrixyl Synthe'6.

Mechanism, in plain English

When collagen degrades, it releases matrikine fragments that signal fibroblasts to make more collagen. Matrixyl mimics this signal. The KTTKS sequence activates genes for collagen I, III, and fibronectin. The palmitic acid tail helps penetrate the skin barrier. In vitro, Matrixyl nearly doubles collagen synthesis versus controls.

What the evidence shows

Key study (Lintner, 2002): wrinkle depth reduced up to 68% over 4 months. Additional studies confirm increased collagen I and III, improved skin thickness and elasticity. Matrixyl 3000 showed comparable results to retinol with better tolerability in some comparisons. Limitation: most clinical data is manufacturer-funded.

FDA status

Cosmetic ingredient, not regulated as a drug by the FDA.

Common synonyms

Matrixyl, palmitoyl pentapeptide-4, Pal-KTTKS, Matrixyl 3000, Matrixyl Synthe'6

Safety and adverse-event landscape

Excellent topical safety. Non-irritating in patch testing. No systemic absorption concerns at cosmetic concentrations. Well-tolerated by sensitive skin.
